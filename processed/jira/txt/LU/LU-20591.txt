--- Comment (JIRAUSER21604, Mon, 17 Aug 2026 20:42:55 +0000) ---
> How do you know the highest inode and dnode numbers?

We actually don't know the highest numbers and we don't use them. Threads take fixed-size chunks of the object-number space from a shared atomic cursor. A chunk whose load()/next() returns 1 found nothing at or after its start; chunks are handed out in increasing order, so every later chunk is empty too — that thread sets a global done and the others finish their current chunk. No size query, no maximum, same logic on both backends.

 

You're right that static equal ranges distribute badly, and we hit exactly that: on a young MDT 2M inodes sat in 62 of ~1000 groups, so an even split leaves N−1 threads idle. With dynamic chunks a thread that lands on an unallocated region walks the UNINIT groups in microseconds and takes the next chunk. On ldiskfs it's worth making the chunk a multiple of inodes-per-group, so shards are group-aligned and two threads never hold the same group bitmap.

> we can't make it OSD agnostic

The sharding rides on load(hash)/store() — the position cookies both OSDs already implement for scrub checkpointing. The consumer loop is identical on both: init(DOIF_PARALLEL) → load(start-1) → next()/rec() until store() >= end → fini(). It never interprets the cookie, only compares and advances, so "inode number" vs "dnode number" never reaches it. That's running on both backends today.

One osd-zfs quirk found doing it: load(hash) resumes at hash + 1 because dmu_object_next() returns objects strictly after *objectp, so a checkpoint/resume silently skips one object. Private iterators start the search at hash instead.

> ldiskfs is already fast enough with single thread

Agreed, and our numbers say the same. With block parsing one private thread is 6.87M obj/s warm; cold — the regime that actually sizes a scan — it is 1,119,013 against 1,420,664 at four threads, only 1.27×, because one thread already reaches ~78% of an NVMe stripe and four reach 99%. Parallelism is not what ldiskfs needs.

ZFS is where it pays: 97,304 singleton → 410,529 at eight threads cold, 4.2×, and that is what makes the in-kernel scanner ~1.8× the userspace one there. So the backend that's harder to shard is the one that needs it — which is why the range-free chunking above is the part worth keeping.
