--- Description ---
Concept

	Improve write performance for single shared file. It anticipates patterns such as unaligned or strided access, but it is not limited to those. Instead of increasing bandwidth through higher parallelism, high bandwidth is achieved through consistently low latency. In other words, high bandwidth can be attained even with 1 client process per node.

	Reducing the cost of canceling extent locks. Unaligned DIO achieves high bandwidth with large writes or many client processes by avoiding lock cancellation through server-side extent locks. However, unlike Buffered I/O (BIO), write() latency cannot be hidden. To escape this trade-off, a buffering mechanism separate from the client's page cache is required. (Sorry, I'm reconsidering it now....)

	This design aims to remove the write-back process from the critical path of ownership transfer when an extent lock is cancelled.

Anticipated Q&A

Q. Why not use LDLM lock ahead?

A. Lock ahead is a valuable approach and works well when the access pattern is known in advance. However, it does not preserve the speculative extension of extent locks. For strided writes to a shared file, acquiring a range that covers hundreds of write() calls in a single request is a substantial benefit, and I would like to retain it.

Q. Why not build on folio support?

A. Folio support is an important improvement and should benefit many workloads. For shared-file writes specifically, though, my understanding is that the main bottleneck is the cost of handing over extent locks between clients, which folios are not designed to address. I see this proposal as complementary to the folio work rather than an alternative to it.

Q. Why not rely on unaligned DIO?

A. Unaligned DIO performs very well with large transfer sizes or many client processes, and it is a good fit for those cases. This proposal targets a different scenario: the hypothesis is that consistently low write() latency leads to shorter end-to-end runtimes for real applications. The goal is therefore to deliver good performance even when parallelism is modest and transfer sizes are small.

 

Evaluated prototype using ior hard, excluding write latency to ldiskfs (via FEMU's NoSSD mode).

I'm preparing a paper on this work. Any feedback is welcome.

--- Comment (paf0186, Wed, 30 Sep 2026 18:01:37 +0000) ---
Sohei,

For data, visibility and ordering are the same thing - it’s basically visibility that we order.

If you’re ordering reads and writes, then obviously you must have rigid ordering AND visibility or the reads will get incorrect data.

For writes vs writes, there’s no visibility per-se, but you still have to ensure ordering - last write wins.  In theory you could discard earlier writes on overlap, but that’s a small optimization with lots of complexity and it still requires locking to notice it.

It’s just not possible to have distributed asynchronicity or caching without locking to ensure consistency.  This is a general theorem, not a contingent fact about Lustre; it applies to CPU caches just as much.  If you create another buffer on the client, it doesn’t change this - UDIO does this, but it must use that buffer synchronously.

First, I'd like to focus on the ranges of the extents and the locks. Consider a write by client A to [0, 47008), write(A), followed by a write by client B to [47008, 94016), write(B). These two writes have no real dependency on each other. However, if A holds a lock covering [0, EOF), current Lustre creates a dependency between them. (The reason is speculative lock extension, which lets buffered I/O complete write() without any communication as long as the write falls within the lock's range.) In principle, the metadata describing the extents, [0, 47008) and [47008, 94016), which is only a few bytes, should be sufficient to determine the dependency relationship between the two writes, and the dirty data transfer latency (from receiving the blocking AST until the cancel) should not need to sit between write(A) and write(B).

(Note: this isn't a precise example, since lock ranges are actually rounded to page boundaries. Also, in actual ior-hard writeback, far more than 47008 bytes of dirty data ends up in that latency, because before write(B), A has also written [94016, 141024) and so on.)

And my question goes a step further... Consider a write by client A' to [0, 47008), write(A'), followed by a write by client B' to [0, 47008), write(B'). While there is a genuine dependency here, surely there is no need for the dirty data transfer latency to sit between write(A') and write(B')?

--- Comment (JIRAUSER19217, Thu, 1 Oct 2026 00:20:15 +0000) ---
The current prototype works roughly as follows. When a client receives a blocking AST, it stops accepting new writes under that extent lock and sends the server metadata describing its buffered writes, such as their byte ranges. Once the server has recorded those writes and established any necessary ordering between overlapping writes, the client releases the lock without waiting for the data itself to be transferred. The old client keeps the buffered data and continues sending it asynchronously after losing the lock.

--- Comment (JIRAUSER19217, Thu, 1 Oct 2026 16:42:20 +0000) ---
Setting aside the question of whether it takes the specific form of a "DLM lock", there are two mechanisms for synchronization: 1) a client-side mechanism (=DLM lock) that determines a ordering and supports lock expansion, and 2) a server-side mechanism (maybe not DLM lock) that resolves dependencies regarding actual byte-range reads and writes. Mechanism 2) constructs a graph (DAG) based on byte-range dependencies and the epochs assigned by mechanism 1) (where the epoch value is simply incremented each time ownership is transferred).
